Should I launch a waitlist or keep building?

The real question is whether anyone wants this — and building for another month does not produce that answer. A waitlist does, in a day.

Launch a waitlist or keep building is the wrong question, and it is the one almost every pre-launch founder gets stuck on. The real question is whether you have enough evidence that anyone wants this — and building longer does not produce that evidence.

The short answer
  • Waitlist, almost always — it costs a day and produces the only data that matters
  • A waitlist is not a launch. It is a test of whether the description is compelling
  • The number to watch is not signups, it is how many reply when you email them
  • Keep building only if the product cannot demonstrate anything at all yet

"It is not ready" usually means "I am not ready", and that does not improve with another month.

Why "is it ready?" is unanswerable

Readiness has no threshold. There is always another feature, another edge case, another polish pass — and every one of them feels like the last thing standing between you and launch.

The reason the question never resolves is that you are the wrong person to answer it. You know every flaw. A prospective customer knows none of them and is comparing you to a spreadsheet, not to your mental image of the finished product.

Replace it with an answerable question: can someone get one useful outcome from this today? Not the full workflow. One outcome, once. If yes, you are past the readiness threshold that matters.

What a waitlist actually tests

Not demand for your product. Demand for your description of the problem — which is a more useful thing to test at this stage, because if the description does not land, the product cannot save it.

A waitlist page needs four things and nothing else: the problem in the customer's language, who it is for, what it replaces, and an email field. No feature list, no screenshots you are embarrassed by, no launch date.

The description work here is the same work as positioning, done early and cheaply. Most founders do it after launch, when it is expensive.

The number that matters

Signups are the vanity metric of waitlists. Two hundred emails from a launch post means almost nothing — people subscribe to things at near-zero cost and forget within a day.

The real test comes next. Email everyone on the list one question: what are you using for this today? Then count replies.

Ten replies describing the same painful workaround is worth more than a thousand unengaged signups, and it is the input you need for setting a price before launch.

When to keep building instead

Two situations genuinely justify it, and neither is "it does not feel ready".

The product cannot produce one useful outcome yet. If nobody could get anything out of it even with you sitting beside them, a waitlist is premature — you have nothing to describe. Build the single path that produces one outcome, then stop.

The demo would actively damage trust. For products handling money, health data or client records, a visibly broken first impression is expensive with exactly the professional audience you want. Even then: waitlist page yes, product demo no.

Everything else — missing features, unpolished design, incomplete settings — is not a reason to wait. It is a reason to be clear about what the early version does.

A test worth being honest about: if you were told you had to put the page up tomorrow, could you? If the answer is yes and you still are not doing it, the blocker is not the product. Naming that accurately is more useful than another sprint.

The version most founders should run

Waitlist page and continued building, in that order, with one difference: what you build is now decided by what the repliers tell you rather than by what you imagined.

A concrete week: put the page up, share it in the two places your buyers gather, email everyone who signs up with the one question, and interview the first five who reply. Then build for a month knowing something.

The alternative — another month of building on assumptions — costs the same month and produces no new information. That is the entire argument.

Once the waitlist is working

Ship to the repliers before you feel ready. Early users who told you their problem are the most forgiving audience you will ever have, and they convert far better than strangers.

Then run the actual launch as a separate event once you have a handful of paying customers and some evidence — the launch playbook covers that sequence, and it works far better with existing users than from zero.

If the waitlist produces nothing after two attempts in different places, that is real information about the idea rather than the page. Knowing when to kill an idea covers reading that honestly.

Waitlist or keep building FAQ

Should I launch a waitlist or keep building?

A waitlist, in almost every case. It costs a day and tests whether your description of the problem makes someone act, which is the one fact you cannot obtain by building longer. Keep building only if the product cannot produce a single useful outcome yet.

How do I know if my product is ready to launch?

Readiness has no threshold and you are the wrong person to judge it, because you know every flaw and your customer is comparing you to a spreadsheet. Replace the question with: can someone get one useful outcome from this today?

What should a waitlist page include?

Four things: the problem in the customer's language, who it is for, what it replaces, and an email field. No feature list, no screenshots you are unhappy with, and no launch date you might miss.

How many waitlist signups do I need?

Signups are the vanity metric. Email the list one question — what are you using for this today — and count replies. Under 5% means curiosity rather than demand; over 15% means these people have the problem and you should ship to them unfinished.

Is it ever right to keep building instead?

Two cases. If the product cannot produce one useful outcome even with you sitting beside the user, there is nothing to describe yet. And if a broken demo would damage trust — money, health data, client records — hold the demo but still put up the page.
