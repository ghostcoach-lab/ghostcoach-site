SaaS SEO when you are the only writer

It compounds while you sleep, which suits a one-person business perfectly. It also takes two quarters to tell you anything, which does not.

SaaS SEO is the best long-term channel for a solo founder and the worst first one. It compounds while you sleep, which suits a one-person business perfectly — and it takes six to twelve months to tell you whether it is working, which does not.

The short answer
  • Start it early, judge it late — begin at any stage, expect nothing for two quarters
  • Write for decisions, not topics — the queries founders type when stuck
  • One page a week beats four in a burst then nothing for a month
  • Do not make it your only channel until it has produced customers

SEO is a second channel that becomes a first channel eighteen months later.

The timing problem

Every other channel gives you feedback in weeks. SEO gives you feedback in quarters, and that mismatch is what causes solo founders to abandon it at month four — just before it would have started working.

The practical resolution is to run SEO alongside a faster channel rather than instead of one. Cold outreach or community participation produces customers now; SEO produces them later. Running only the slow one means no revenue for two quarters, which most people cannot sustain.

The channel framework covers judging a channel honestly, and time-to-signal is the column that matters most when you are choosing your first.

What to write about

Not "topics in your space". The pages that convert for a small SaaS answer the specific question someone types when they are stuck, and those queries are long, specific and low-competition.

Write thisNot this
"Should I lower my price or fix onboarding?""The ultimate guide to SaaS pricing"
"Churned customers won't answer my survey""10 tips to reduce churn"
"Is it a positioning or a product problem?""What is positioning?"

The left column has less search volume and far higher intent. Someone typing a full sentence about their situation is closer to acting than someone typing two words, and the competition for those phrases is usually a forum thread from 2019.

The right column is where large content teams compete, and you will not outrank them on a head term with one page a week. The persona-level version of this — writing for the moment rather than the topic — is the same logic behind positioning.

The cadence that works

One page a week, indefinitely, beats every other pattern. Four pages in a burst followed by six quiet weeks produces a site that looks abandoned to both readers and crawlers.

For a founder with limited hours, one page a week is roughly three to four hours including research. That is sustainable alongside a job or alongside building, which is the point — a cadence you abandon produces nothing regardless of how good the pages were.

If four hours a week is your entire capacity, SEO is probably not the right channel yet — see growing a SaaS around a full-time job for how to allocate. Once a page is written, where to post your app covers the distribution side — the communities and channels worth the time versus the ones that waste it.

What a page needs

Five things, and none of them requires an SEO tool.

  • A title that matches the query as a person would phrase it
  • The answer in the first two sentences, before any preamble
  • Headings phrased as questions people actually ask
  • Links to three or four related pages on your own site
  • One specific, defensible position rather than a balanced overview

The second point matters more than it used to. AI answer engines lift the direct answer near the top of a page, and a page that buries its conclusion under three paragraphs of context gets skipped in favour of one that does not.

Connecting pages to the product

Every page should end with a next step that follows from the page's own subject. A generic "start your free trial" attached to an article about churn surveys converts poorly because it does not follow from anything.

The pages that convert are the ones where the product is the obvious answer to the question the page just posed. That is a content selection problem — write about the decisions your product actually helps with, and the connection writes itself.

Interlink deliberately. A cluster of eight pages that reference each other outperforms twenty unconnected ones, because internal links are the main signal you control and a page with no inbound links from your own site is invisible.

When to judge it

Not before month six, and then on qualified signups rather than traffic. A page attracting a thousand monthly visitors who never sign up is worth less than one attracting forty who do.

The realistic pattern is nothing for two quarters, then a slow accumulation as individual pages start ranking. If at month nine you have no page producing signups, the problem is usually topic selection — you wrote about your industry rather than about decisions.

SaaS SEO for solo founders FAQ

Is SEO worth it for a solo SaaS founder?

As a long-term channel, yes — it compounds while you sleep, which suits a one-person business. As a first channel, no. It takes six to twelve months to produce signal, so run it alongside something faster rather than instead of one.

What should a solo SaaS founder write about for SEO?

The specific questions people type when they are stuck, phrased as full sentences. Those queries have lower volume and far higher intent than head terms, and the competition is usually an old forum thread rather than a content team.

How often should I publish?

One page a week, indefinitely. That is roughly three to four hours including research, which is sustainable alongside a job. Four pages in a burst followed by six quiet weeks produces a site that looks abandoned.

How long before SaaS SEO works?

Expect nothing for two quarters, then slow accumulation as individual pages begin ranking. Judge it at month six on qualified signups rather than traffic — a page bringing a thousand visitors who never sign up is worth less than one bringing forty who do.

What makes an SEO page convert for SaaS?

Answering the question in the first two sentences, headings phrased as real questions, three or four internal links, one defensible position rather than a balanced overview, and a next step that follows from the page's own subject rather than a generic trial CTA.
