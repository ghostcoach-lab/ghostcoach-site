How to choose between two SaaS ideas

Five tie-breakers, weighted in order. The cost of deliberating is now higher than the cost of picking the wrong one.

When you need to choose between two SaaS ideas, the cost of deliberating quickly exceeds the cost of picking the wrong one — because neither idea is being tested while you go back and forth.

The tie-breakers, in order
  • 1. Distribution — which audience can you reach on Monday without permission?
  • 2. Recurring need — which problem recurs monthly rather than once?
  • 3. Existing spend — which one are people already paying to solve?
  • 4. Time-to-value — which can prove itself in one session?
  • 5. Your unfair knowledge — which one do you understand better than most?

Run both ideas through all five. Whichever wins three of the five, build that one. Decide this week.

Notice what is not on that list: which idea is bigger, which is more interesting, or which has the larger market. Those criteria feel serious and they do not discriminate between two ideas a solo founder can actually execute.

Tie-breaker 1 — Distribution

The single best predictor of whether a solo product survives is whether you can reach its buyers without a budget. Ask a specific question about each idea: where do these people already gather, and can you post there on Monday?

A named forum, subreddit, Slack group, newsletter or conference is a yes. "Small business owners" is a no — that is a demographic, not a place.

An idea with a narrower market you can reach beats a larger market you cannot. This is the tie-breaker that decides most cases, and it is the one founders weight least because market size feels more important than access.

Tie-breaker 2 — Does the problem recur?

Subscription revenue requires a recurring problem. If your product solves something once — a migration, a setup, a one-time conversion — customers finish and leave, and no retention tactic will hold them.

Ask how often each problem occurs for one customer. Weekly or monthly supports a subscription. Annually or once does not, however painful it is.

This is the cause of value churn, and it is structural rather than fixable — the churn reduction framework covers why. Choosing the recurring idea now avoids a problem you cannot solve later.

Tie-breaker 3 — Is anyone already paying?

Existing spend is the strongest demand signal available before you build anything. Not "would you pay" — actual money currently leaving someone's account.

That spend can be a competitor, a freelancer, a consultant, an agency, or staff hours. Any of those proves budget exists and that the problem is worth money to someone.

An idea where nobody currently pays anything is not automatically dead, but it carries a much heavier burden. You would be creating a budget line rather than redirecting one, which is slow work for one person.

Tie-breaker 4 — How fast can it prove itself?

Which idea can demonstrate value in a single session? That question decides your trial mechanics, your onboarding burden and your support load before you have written a line of code.

Products needing a week of setup before anything useful happens are hard for a solo founder to sell, because every stalled setup becomes a support conversation. The onboarding framework covers why activation speed matters more than feature depth at this scale.

Tie-breaker 5 — Your unfair knowledge

Which problem do you understand better than most people who might build for it? Domain knowledge is the most durable advantage available to a solo founder, because it is the one thing a competitor cannot copy in a weekend.

Weight this last, not first. Founders who lead with it end up building for an industry they know well but cannot reach, which fails on tie-breaker one.

Why you cannot build both

Two products means two landing pages, two onboarding flows, two support queues and two audiences to reach — on one person's week.

The result is not two half-businesses. It is two products that each look abandoned to anyone evaluating them, because neither gets the sustained attention that early traction requires.

Shelving one idea is not deleting it. Write it down, note why it lost, and revisit in six months when you have distribution and can evaluate it against a real business rather than another idea.

Set a decision deadline of one week and treat it as binding. The most expensive outcome here is not choosing wrong — it is spending three months choosing, which is long enough to have validated the loser and moved on.

After you have chosen

Do not start building. Spend the first week talking to people who have the problem — customer interviews at this stage cost nothing and change what you build.

Then work through validating the idea properly before committing months to it. If the validation goes badly, knowing when to kill it matters more than persistence.

Once the idea survives validation, positioning is the next decision — and it determines your pricing ceiling. The positioning framework covers naming the alternative you replace.

Choosing between SaaS ideas FAQ

How do I choose between two SaaS ideas?

Score both against five tie-breakers: whether you can reach the buyers without permission, whether the problem recurs monthly, whether anyone already pays to solve it, whether it proves itself in one session, and whether you understand it better than most. Three yeses out of five wins.

Should I pick the SaaS idea with the bigger market?

No. Market size does not discriminate usefully between two ideas a solo founder can execute. An idea with a narrower audience you can reach on Monday beats a larger market you have no access to, because distribution is the binding constraint.

Can I build two SaaS ideas at once?

Not effectively. Two products means two landing pages, two onboarding flows, two support queues and two audiences on one person's week. The result is two products that each look abandoned to anyone evaluating them.

How long should I spend deciding between SaaS ideas?

One week, treated as binding. The most expensive outcome is not choosing wrong — it is spending three months deciding, which is long enough to have validated the losing idea and moved on to the next thing.

What if both SaaS ideas score the same?

Pick the one that scored yes on distribution. If they tie there too, the ideas are close enough that the choice does not matter, and picking either today beats picking the better one next month.
