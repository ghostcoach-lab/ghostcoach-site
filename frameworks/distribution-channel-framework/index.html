How to pick one acquisition channel and stick to it

Standard channel frameworks assume parallel testing and budget. You can test one at a time, and each test costs a month — which changes the whole method.

A distribution channel framework normally tells you to test nineteen acquisition channels in parallel and double down on the winner. You can test one at a time, and each test costs you a month. That single constraint changes the entire method.

The short answer
  • Pick one channel, run it for ninety days, define the success number before you start
  • Choose it by where your first three customers came from, not by theory
  • Test sequentially — three channels run badly produce no information about any of them
  • Judge on qualified signups, not traffic or followers

The most common solo acquisition failure is running three channels at a third of the required effort.

Why parallel testing does not transfer

The Bullseye method — list nineteen channels, test several cheaply, focus on the one that works — is sound advice for a team with budget. It assumes you can run cheap tests simultaneously and read the results independently.

Alone, three things break. Every channel needs sustained effort before it produces signal, so a "cheap test" is really a month. Your channels are not independent, because they all draw on the same pool of your hours. And a half-run channel produces a result you cannot interpret: no signups from a channel you gave 30% effort tells you nothing about whether it works.

The solo version is sequential and slower, and it produces conclusions you can actually rely on.

Picking your first acquisition channel

Do not choose theoretically. Get your first three customers by any means available, then look backwards at where they came from and formalise that.

Founders who pick a channel from a list commit six months before knowing whether their buyers are reachable that way. Founders who follow their first customers backwards start with a channel already proven to contain their audience — the $0 to $1k playbook covers that manual early phase.

If you have no customers yet, the tiebreaker is where you can already reach people without permission: a community you are part of, a list you already have, or a place your buyers publicly discuss the problem.

The ninety-day test

Four rules that make the result readable.

Define success first. A specific number of qualified signups per month, decided before you start. Without it, you will rationalise whatever happens.

Run it at full effort. Whatever the channel demands weekly, do that. A channel run at half effort produces a half-signal, which is worse than no signal because it feels like data.

Do not add a second channel mid-test. The moment you do, you cannot attribute anything and the ninety days are wasted.

Judge on qualified signups. Not traffic, impressions or followers. A channel producing 4,000 visitors and two trials has failed; one producing 90 visitors and eight trials has worked.

Reading the result

Three outcomes, three responses.

It hit the number. Do not add a channel. Increase the volume of what worked until it stops scaling, which usually takes longer than founders expect.

It missed but produced something. Run one more cycle with a specific change, not a general improvement. If the second cycle also misses, move on.

It produced nothing. Move to the next channel. And check whether the problem is really the channel — if visitors arrive and nobody signs up, the constraint is positioning and every channel will fail identically.

Before blaming a channel, check your conversion from visitor to signup. A channel cannot be evaluated through a broken landing page, and swapping channels to fix a conversion problem is the most expensive way to avoid rewriting a headline.

When to add a second channel

When the first one is producing predictably and you have hours left over. Predictably means you can state roughly how many customers it produces per month and why.

Most solo products can reach $10k MRR on a single well-run channel. Adding a second before the first is systematised usually halves both. Distribution channel framework FAQ

How should a solo founder choose an acquisition channel?

Get your first three customers by any means, then look backwards at where they came from and formalise that. Choosing theoretically from a list commits you to six months before you know whether your buyers are reachable that way.

Why does the Bullseye framework not work for solo founders?

It assumes you can run several cheap tests in parallel and read them independently. Alone, every channel needs sustained effort before producing signal, all channels draw on the same pool of your hours, and a half-run channel produces a result you cannot interpret.

How long should I test an acquisition channel?

Ninety days at full effort, with the success number defined before you start. Time to signal varies by channel — direct outreach shows results in two to four weeks, while SEO can take six to twelve months, which makes it a poor first channel.

Should I run more than one channel at once?

Not until the first produces predictably. Three channels run at a third of the required effort produce no usable information about any of them, and this is the most common acquisition failure at solo scale.

How do I judge whether a channel worked?

On qualified signups, not traffic or followers. A channel producing 4,000 visitors and two trials has failed; one producing 90 visitors and eight trials has worked. Define the specific number before you start or you will rationalise whatever happens.

What if no channel seems to work?

Check your visitor-to-signup conversion before blaming the channel. If people arrive and nobody signs up, the constraint is positioning, and every channel will fail in the same way until that is fixed.
