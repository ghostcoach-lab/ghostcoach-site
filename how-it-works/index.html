How it works

Four steps from sign-up to a real recommendation.

You answer the onboarding questions once. After that Marcus knows your product, your stage, your bottleneck and your 90-day goal — so every session starts with context instead of a re-introduction.

01

Build your business profile

Create an account and answer the onboarding questions in plain language. What you built, where you are, the one thing most in the way, and the 90-day goal you are working toward. This happens once, before your first session.

Takes 5 minutes, once
02

Marcus diagnoses before it advises

Your profile answers the basics, so Marcus spends its questions on what actually changes the recommendation. For pricing, what your product replaces. For churn, when in the lifecycle users leave. The diagnosis comes before the advice, every time.

Sharp, specific questions
03

You get one recommendation

Not a list of options with pros and cons. Marcus picks one, explains why, and tells you when the alternative would be better. "Set your annual plan at 2 months free — that is 8×1 pricing" not "consider annual discounts."

One recommendation, not a menu
04

Every session ends with one action

Marcus closes every coaching session with exactly one concrete action you can take today — or one clarifying question if it needs more context first. No homework lists. No vague next steps. One thing that moves the 90-day goal.

Action, not advice
The framework

Every recommendation maps to one of five pillars.

Marcus built the Ghost OS with decades of business coaching built in. When you bring a problem, Marcus tells you which pillar it belongs to — and sometimes, which pillar the real problem belongs to.

1
Offer Architecture
How your product is packaged, priced, and positioned. Freemium or paid? Monthly or annual? One plan or three? Sets your revenue ceiling before a single customer signs up.
2
AI Delivery Stack
How AI runs your operations — onboarding, support, renewals — so you are never the bottleneck in your own product. The leverage most solo operators leave on the table.
3
Acquisition System
Repeatable, owned channels that compound. Not launch events. A system that brings in strangers every week without paid ads or a sales team.
4
Automation Layer
Billing flows, churn alerts, trial follow-up, onboarding sequences. The connective tissue that lets the business run while you keep building.
5
Revenue Protection
Trial-to-paid conversion, churn reduction, expansion revenue. The highest-leverage growth lever most solo builders ignore until it is too late.

First, a pushback: 3 out of 40 (7.5%) is not a bad conversion rate for a no-card trial. So I'd be wary of treating this as a conversion problem. Your bigger constraints are probably volume (40 trials means each extra point of conversion is 0.4 customers) and activation (whether trial users ever get to the moment the product proves itself).

1. Find out where the 37 drop off.
For invoicing, the activation moment is almost certainly "sent a real invoice to a real client." If a freelancer signs up with no invoice due in the next two weeks, a 14-day clock is working against you. Check how many of the 37 actually sent one. If most didn't, your problem is activation, not pricing or the pitch.

2. Talk to the non-converters personally.
With 37 a month, you can email every one of them. Something short and human: "I'm the founder, what stopped you?" Even 5 replies will tell you more than any funnel tweak.

3. Make the trial about usage, not days.
Trial ends after their first sent invoice plus some period, or extend it if they haven't sent one. Pair that with a couple of plain-text emails triggered by behavior: "You've added a client but haven't sent an invoice yet, want help?"

4. Top of funnel, no budget:

  • A free invoice generator or template page for freelance designers (SEO plus a natural upsell to tracking payments)
  • Being genuinely useful in designer communities (Reddit, Dribbble/Figma communities, designer Slacks), not dropping links
  • Partnering with designers who teach freelancing (course creators, newsletter writers), maybe with an affiliate cut
  • Asking your 3 paying customers where they found you, then doing more of that

Things I'd resist:

  • Requiring a card up front. It'll improve conversion percentage but cut signups, and at 40 a month you can't afford that trade.
  • Cutting the price. $29 is already modest. If people don't see value, $19 won't fix it.
  • Building more features. Unless the interviews point to a specific gap, that's a comfortable way to avoid the harder distribution work.

Also worth a look: what's your churn on those 3 paying customers? If they stick around, your lifetime value may justify more patience than the raw numbers suggest.

You don't need more signups right now. You need to convert more of the 40 people already trying your product.

Your numbers tell an interesting story:

  • 40 – Monthly trial signups
  • 3 – Monthly paid conversions
  • 7.5% – Trial-to-paid conversion
  • $87 – New monthly recurring revenue

What happens if you improve conversion?

  • 7.5% (current): 3 new customers, $87 new MRR
  • 15%: 6 new customers, $174 new MRR
  • 20%: 8 new customers, $232 new MRR
  • 25%: 10 new customers, $290 new MRR

These are new MRR figures, not total accumulated revenue. But notice something important: doubling conversion doubles revenue without acquiring a single extra visitor.

I'd approach this as a 30-day experiment, not a massive marketing project.

1. Find out why people aren't paying

This is your highest-priority task.

You have approximately 37 people each month who try your product but don't pay. That's a lot of valuable feedback going unused.

Personally email your last 10–15 trial users who didn't convert.

Ask:

  • What were you hoping the product would help you accomplish?
  • Did you manage to send an invoice?
  • What stopped you from continuing?
  • What are you currently using instead?

Don't pitch them. Don't defend the product. Just listen.

Look for patterns:

  • They never completed setup.
  • They created an invoice but didn't send it.
  • They didn't have enough clients to justify $29/month.
  • They didn't understand the payment-tracking functionality.
  • They liked it but didn't need it frequently enough.
  • They couldn't find a feature they expected.

Your goal is to identify the biggest reason people don't pay, rather than guessing.

2. Improve the first 10 minutes of the trial

Your product is an invoicing tool. Users should experience its value almost immediately.

Think about the activation journey:

  • 1. Sign up – User creates an account.
  • 2. Set up business details – Add business name, logo, currency and payment details.
  • 3. Create first invoice – Make this as easy as possible, ideally with a sample invoice.
  • 4. Send first invoice – This is the first meaningful value moment.
  • 5. Track payment – Show how the product helps them see outstanding and paid invoices.

I'd measure one metric above all others:

What percentage of trial users send at least one invoice?

For example, if 40 sign up but only 8 send an invoice, your biggest problem probably isn't pricing. It's activation.

Consider adding:

  • A sample invoice users can edit.
  • A pre-filled demo client.
  • A setup checklist.
  • A prominent "Create your first invoice" button.
  • A short welcome email offering help.
3. Use the 14-day trial to sell the outcome

A 14-day trial is fine, but don't just send generic product announcements.

Build a simple email sequence.

Suggested trial email sequence (free to implement):

  • Day 0 — Welcome: Help them create their first invoice.
  • Day 1 — Quick win: Show how to send an invoice in under five minutes.
  • Day 3 — Feature education: Demonstrate how to track outstanding payments.
  • Day 5 — Personal check-in: Ask whether they've managed to send their first invoice.
  • Day 8 — Value: Explain how the product helps reduce chasing late payments and administrative work.
  • Day 11 — Objection handling: Address common questions about pricing, setup and payment tracking.
  • Day 13 — Trial ending: Remind them their trial is ending and explain what they'll retain with a paid plan.
  • Day 14 — Trial ended: Give them a clear upgrade option and an invitation to reply if something stopped them.

Keep the emails short, personal and useful. As a solo founder, you can make them feel like they're hearing from a real person rather than a marketing department.

4. Personally onboard a few users

This is probably your biggest competitive advantage as a solo founder.

You don't need an automated onboarding system yet.

When someone signs up, send a personal message:

"Hey! Thanks for trying [Product].

I'm the founder and I'm curious what brought you here. Are you looking to create invoices faster, keep track of outstanding payments, or something else?

Happy to help you get your first invoice set up if you'd like."

Offer a 10-minute onboarding call to people who seem interested.

Even if only five people accept, you'll learn more about your product and customers than you might from months of guessing.

I'd also identify users who:

  • Created multiple invoices.
  • Added clients.
  • Returned several times.
  • Viewed payment tracking.

These are your most promising prospects. Reach out personally before their trial ends.

5. Get customers without advertising

I'd focus on three organic channels rather than trying to be everywhere.

A. Freelance designer communities

Reddit: Participate in communities like r/freelance, r/graphic_design and relevant designer subreddits. Answer questions about invoicing, late payments and running a freelance business.

Indie Hackers: Share your experience building an invoicing product. Ask designers what frustrates them about getting paid.

LinkedIn: Connect with freelance graphic designers and independent design studios. Publish practical tips about invoicing and payment collection.

Important: Don't just drop links to your product. Become genuinely useful in these communities.

B. Create content that attracts people searching for solutions

You have a very specific audience, which is great for SEO.

I'd start with these five articles:

  • 1. How to invoice freelance graphic design clients.
  • 2. How much should freelance designers charge?
  • 3. How to politely chase clients for overdue invoices.
  • 4. What should a freelance design invoice include?
  • 5. Best invoicing software for freelance graphic designers.

Include screenshots of your product where relevant.

The fourth and fifth articles are particularly interesting because they attract people who may already be looking for an invoicing solution.

C. Build referral relationships

Find people who already serve freelance designers:

  • Graphic design educators.
  • Freelance design coaches.
  • Design newsletter owners.
  • YouTube creators teaching freelancing.
  • Communities for independent designers.

Offer them a free account and a recurring referral commission for customers they introduce.

You don't need a big affiliate programme. Start with three people.

6. Don't immediately change your pricing

$29/month isn't obviously unreasonable for software that helps someone manage invoices and collect payments.

But you need to communicate the financial value.

For example:

Instead of: "Professional invoicing and payment tracking for freelancers."

Try: "Spend less time chasing clients for money. Create professional invoices and see exactly who's paid and who still owes you."

You're selling less administrative work and better visibility over cash flow.

One thing I'd investigate: do your customers invoice regularly enough to justify $29 every month?

If many designers only invoice one or two clients occasionally, a lower-priced annual plan or a less expensive tier might help.

But don't introduce discounts before understanding why people aren't buying.

7. Your 30-day action plan

Here's what I'd actually do if I were running this business alone.

30-day founder growth sprint

Week 1 — Understand the problem

  • Email 10–15 former trial users
  • Interview at least 5 designers
  • Identify the biggest onboarding drop-off
  • Measure how many trial users send an invoice

Week 2 — Improve activation

  • Simplify first invoice creation
  • Add a sample invoice or demo data
  • Create a simple onboarding checklist
  • Write the first 3 trial emails

Week 3 — Find organic customers

  • Contribute useful answers in 3 designer communities
  • Publish 2 helpful articles
  • Contact 5 design educators or creators
  • Personally onboard 5 trial users

Week 4 — Measure and improve

  • Review trial-to-invoice activation
  • Review trial-to-paid conversion by cohort
  • Contact non-converting users for feedback
  • Fix the biggest remaining conversion problem
8. What numbers should you aim for?

I'd set these as initial internal targets, not promises or universal industry benchmarks:

  • Monthly signups: currently 40, initial target 40–50
  • Trial-to-paid: currently 7.5%, initial target 12–15%
  • Paying customers/month: currently 3, initial target 5–7
  • New MRR/month: currently $87, initial target $145–$203

Track cohorts consistently, too: give each signup cohort enough time to finish its trial and convert.

My biggest recommendation

If I were you, I'd spend the next month doing just three things:

  • 1. Talk to customers. Find out why 37 people aren't paying.
  • 2. Improve activation. Get more trial users to send their first invoice.
  • 3. Do founder-led sales. Personally help promising trial users succeed.

Don't spend the next month building ten new features, redesigning your website or trying five social media platforms.

You need to understand the gap between someone thinking your product is interesting and someone deciding it's worth $29 every month.

Three questions that would change my advice

I'd like to understand a little more about your actual funnel.

  • 1. How do people currently find your product? Google, Reddit, Product Hunt, social media, referrals, or something else?
  • 2. What percentage of trial users actually create and send at least one invoice? If you don't know, that's fine.
  • 3. Are your 3 paying customers still subscribed after 2–3 months, or do they cancel quickly?

Those three answers will help distinguish whether you have an acquisition problem, an activation problem, or a product/value problem.

And that's important because each requires a very different solution.
